On **3 June 1960**, Lt. Gen. Bernard A. Schriever, commander of the Air Force Ballistic Missile Division, announced the creation of **The Aerospace Corporation** at a press conference in El Segundo, California. Incorporated as a California nonprofit, it was established to provide independent scientific and technical oversight of the Air Force's missile and space programmes — functions that could not, for conflict-of-interest reasons, remain inside a for-profit defence contractor.

## Origin: The STL Problem

The corporation grew directly out of a congressional finding. **Space Technology Laboratories (STL)** — the technical brains behind the Atlas, Thor, and Titan ICBMs — had been operating as a subsidiary of **TRW** (itself formed in 1958 when Ramo-Wooldridge merged with Thompson Products). STL was simultaneously serving as the Air Force's independent technical advisor *and* as a for-profit participant in the programmes it was overseeing, creating an irreconcilable conflict of interest.

In September 1959, **House Report 1121** formally recommended that STL be converted into a nonprofit institution. The Aerospace Corporation was the result. It was staffed with nearly half of STL's engineers and scientists, and by the end of 1960 had purchased STL's research and development campus on El Segundo Boulevard and employed over **1,700 people**, a third of whom were scientists or engineers.

## Role as an FFRDC

As a **Federally Funded Research and Development Centre (FFRDC)**, The Aerospace Corporation occupies a unique structural position: it is funded overwhelmingly by government contracts but operates independently of both the civil service and the commercial market. Its mandate is to deliver objective technical analysis that no profit-motivated contractor could credibly provide.

The corporation's primary customer remains the US Air Force (later Space Force), and it advises on national security satellite systems, launch vehicles, and space domain awareness. It operates the **Space Systems Development Department** and manages classified programmes at facilities with appropriate clearances.

## Significance in UAP Research

Independent researcher **UAP Gerb** places The Aerospace Corporation at **Level 3** in his proposed hierarchy of alleged UFO legacy programme management — alongside RAND, MITRE, Sandia National Laboratories, Los Alamos, and Oak Ridge — positioned above the prime contractors (Northrop, Lockheed, Boeing, SAIC) but below the DoD/IC programme administrators.

The argument is structural: FFRDCs have deep classified access, long institutional continuity, and operate outside normal congressional procurement oversight, making them plausible custodians of long-running sensitive programmes.

**Stephanie O'Sullivan** — a former TRW engineer (c.1982 to late 1980s) who went on to serve at the Office of Naval Intelligence, as CIA DS&T Director, and as Principal Deputy Director of National Intelligence (2011–2017), and who is now chair of the Aerospace Corporation board — was identified in **David Grusch's later media interviews** (Megyn Kelly interview, late 2025) and in a **Liberation Times report (January 2026)** as having managed the crash retrieval issue alongside former DNI James Clapper at ODNI. Her career arc — TRW → naval intelligence → CIA → ODNI → Aerospace Corporation — traces exactly the network of institutions that UAP researchers identify as core to the alleged legacy programme.
