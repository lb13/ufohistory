**Project Grudge** succeeded [Project Sign](/events/1947-12-30-project-sign-established) in February 1949 after the controversy surrounding Sign's suppressed *[Estimate of the Situation](/events/1948-09-estimate-of-the-situation)*. Where Sign had treated UFO reports as a genuine intelligence puzzle, Grudge operated — in the words of later critics — as a debunking operation rather than an investigation.

## Mandate and Methods
Project Grudge reviewed approximately **244 UFO sighting reports** accumulated since 1947. In contrast to Sign's methodology, Grudge evaluated reports on the premise that UFOs *could not* exist, attributing sightings to:
- Conventional aircraft
- Weather balloons
- Stars, planets, and meteors
- Optical illusions
- Solar reflections
- "Large hailstones" (cited in at least one case)

Internal critics noted that standard intelligence procedures were abandoned: little or no field investigation was conducted while the project simultaneously claimed all reports were thoroughly reviewed.

## Public Relations Campaign
Grudge launched a proactive public-relations effort to reduce civilian interest in the phenomenon. In co-ordination with the project, Sidney Shallet of the *Saturday Evening Post* — one of the most widely read magazines of the era — published an article intended to debunk flying saucer reports. The strategy backfired: Shallet noted in passing that a minority of sightings defied analysis, and this detail attracted further media interest rather than suppressing it.

## Final Report (August 1949)
Grudge issued its sole formal report in August 1949 — over **600 pages** in length — concluding:

> *"There is no evidence that objects reported upon are the result of an advanced scientific foreign development; and, therefore they constitute no direct threat to the national security."*

The report recommended that the investigation be *reduced in scope*, on the rationale that the mere existence of an Air Force study encouraged belief in UFOs and contributed to "war hysteria."

## Termination
The Air Force announced the termination of Project Grudge on **27 December 1949**.

## Internal Backlash
The Fort Monmouth radar-visual incident of September 1951 — in which highly trained Signal Corps radar operators tracked a fast-moving UAP — highlighted the inadequacy of Grudge's debunking approach. When General **Charles P. Cabell** (Director of Intelligence, HQ USAF) reviewed the accumulated Grudge records, he reportedly characterised the 1949 Grudge report as **"tripe"** and ordered a fundamental overhaul.

By late 1951, several influential USAF generals had become sufficiently dissatisfied with the state of UAP investigation to dismantle Grudge entirely. It was replaced by [Project Blue Book](/events/1952-project-blue-book) in March 1952, initially under Capt. Edward J. Ruppelt, who brought a markedly more rigorous approach.

## Legacy
Project Grudge established the institutional template that would persist in US government UAP policy for decades: attribute sightings to mundane causes wherever possible, discourage public reporting, and avoid physical investigation. The [Robertson Panel (1953)](/events/1953-robertson-panel) and subsequent regulations — [AFR 200-2](/events/1953-regulation-200-2) and [JANAP 146](/events/1953-regulation-146) — codified and extended this approach.
